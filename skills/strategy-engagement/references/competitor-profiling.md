# Competitor Profiling

## What it is

A structured picture of a competitor built from public and legitimately obtained sources: what it sells, to whom, at what price, how it makes money, what it is investing in, and how it is likely to react to the client's move. It answers "what will this competitor do, and where is it weak?"

## When to use

- The client plans a move (price change, new offer, entry) that a competitor could answer.
- A market entry decision depends on how strong incumbents are.
- An acquisition screen needs a first view of targets.

## When NOT to use

- **The market has dozens of small players.** Profile the structure with [Five Forces](five-forces.md) and share data instead of individual firms.
- **The question is about customers, not rivals.** If customers decide on criteria where all competitors look alike, run [Customer Segmentation](customer-segmentation.md) first.
- **The only useful information is confidential.** If the profile would need a competitor's internal cost data, contracts or plans, stop. Use a should-cost estimate, public signals, and state the gap.
- **A one-off fact is needed.** A competitor's list price for one product does not need a full profile.

## Step by step

1. **Pick competitors** the customer actually compares with the client, usually two to four. Ask the user and check in win/loss data. Output: competitor list with why each matters.
2. **Set the questions** the decision needs answered: price position, service level, cost position, capacity, strategy, likely response. Output: question list.
3. **Gather from legitimate sources only.**
   - Annual reports, registry filings, investor material, press releases.
   - Websites, product catalogues, published price lists, distributor offers.
   - Job postings (hiring signals), patents, trade fair material, trade press.
   - Customers and distributors describing their own experience with the competitor.
   - The client's sales team and win/loss records.
   Output: fact base with source and date per fact.
4. **Respect the ethical limits.** No pretexting (posing as a customer or student to get information). No confidential documents, even if offered. No questioning former competitor employees about non-public matters; they are bound by confidentiality. No asking customers to share competitor contracts or quotes. If in doubt, leave it out and note the gap.
5. **Build the profile.** Offer, customers, price, channels, financials, investments, leadership, recent moves. Mark each item as fact, estimate or inference. Output: profile table.
6. **Infer strategy and likely response.** From investments and public statements, what is the competitor trying to do? What would it do if the client moved? Output: response scenarios with signals to watch.
7. **Draw the implication** for the client's decision in one sentence.

## Inputs needed

- The client's planned move and the decision at stake.
- Competitor names and the client's own win/loss and sales knowledge (user-supplied).
- Public documents on each competitor, or a note that none are available offline and the user must supply them.

## Output produced

- Profile per competitor with sources, likely response to the client's move, signposts to watch.

## Common mistakes

- **Encyclopaedia profiles.** Ten pages of history and product lists with no link to the decision. Partners ask "so what?" and turn the page.
- **Inference stated as fact.** "Competitor X is losing money in service" from one job posting. Label inference and its basis.
- **Static view.** A profile of today without the moves in progress (hiring, investments, acquisitions).
- **Assuming no response.** Business cases that model the client's move as if competitors stand still.
- **Grey-zone sourcing.** Information that looks confidential discredits the whole work and exposes the client. It is never worth it.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Alder Pumps plans a service contract with 48-hour response. Main competitors: Vellmar Pumpen and Ostra Fluid (both fictional), each priced about 6% above Alder.

Vellmar: annual report shows service at a rising share of revenue. Its career site lists 30 open service technician roles in DACH over six months. A trade-press interview with its CEO names "uptime contracts" as a priority. Distributors report Vellmar offering 72-hour response today. Inference: Vellmar is building toward a contract offer; it could match 48 hours within 12 to 24 months.

Ostra: registry filings show flat revenue and falling margin. No service hiring, product launches focused on energy efficiency. Inference: unlikely to answer on service, likely to answer on price in tenders.

An Alder salesperson offered a Vellmar service contract that a customer had forwarded. The team declined to use it and recorded the decision.

Implication: Alder's lead on response time is temporary. Launch in 2027 and lock in multi-year contracts in segment A before Vellmar's offer arrives. Signposts: Vellmar technician headcount, any public launch of a response guarantee.
