# Market Entry

## Typical questions

Clients often arrive with the market already chosen and ask how to enter. The first job is to reopen "whether" without losing the client's trust.

- "How do we enter <country / segment>?" → "Should we enter <market> by <year>, and if so how, given that it must deliver at least EUR <x> revenue and <y> margin within <capital> and <time>?"
- "Our competitor just entered <market>. Should we follow?" → "Is <market> attractive for us on its own merits, and what do we lose if we wait <n> years?"
- "Which of these five countries first?" → A ranking question. Define the criteria and their weights with the client before scoring.
- "We want to sell <product> to <new segment>." → Check whether the segment has the problem the product solves, and whether the existing channel reaches it.

An entry question is sharp when it names a minimum size worth the effort, a time frame and the capital the client will commit. Without a minimum size, every market looks worth entering.

## Issue tree skeleton

A MECE starting tree. Adapt, never use unchanged.

- **Is the market attractive?**
  - Size and growth of the addressable segment, not the whole market. *H: addressable segment is at least EUR <x>m and growing.*
  - Profit pool and structure: concentration, buyer power, substitutes, entry barriers. *H: no player holds more than <y>% share.*
  - Regulatory, certification and local-content hurdles
- **Can we win there?**
  - Customer need: do target customers have the job our offer does, and how do they solve it today?
  - Competitive position: our advantage against incumbents on price, product, service, channel
  - Capabilities: sales, service, supply chain, language and approvals in the new market
- **How would we enter?**
  - Mode: export via distributor, own sales office, partnership or joint venture, acquisition (if allowed)
  - Sequence: which segment or region first, what proof point before scaling
- **Does it pay?**
  - Revenue ramp, margin, investment, payback, value against the cost of capital
  - Downside: what we lose if it fails, and how fast we can exit
- **Is it better than the alternatives?**
  - Same capital and management attention spent on the core or another lever

The last branch is the one teams forget. Entry competes for money and management time with every other option.

## Key analyses

- **Addressable market sizing.** [Market Sizing](market-sizing.md) top-down and bottom-up, reconciled ([Triangulation](triangulation.md)). Size the segment the client can serve, not the headline market.
- **Market structure.** [Five Forces](five-forces.md) and share data. High concentration among local players is often the fastest kill criterion.
- **Customer need and switching.** Interviews with target buyers ([Interview Guide](interview-guide.md)), [Jobs to Be Done](jobs-to-be-done.md) to understand what they would switch from and why.
- **Competitor profiles.** [Competitor Profiling](competitor-profiling.md) for the top players: price, service model, relationships, likely reaction to an entrant.
- **Entry mode comparison.** Cost, speed, control and reversibility per mode; [Value Chain](value-chain.md) shows which activities must be local.
- **Entry business case.** [Business Case](business-case.md) with a realistic ramp, [Unit Economics](unit-economics.md) per customer or per unit, and [Scenario Planning](scenario-planning.md) for incumbent reaction.

## Traps

- **"1% of a big market".** Assuming a small share of a large market without a bottom-up path to customers. Build the ramp from named customer types, channel capacity and win rates.
- **Home-market transfer.** Assuming customers, channels and price levels work as at home. Test them in the target market before modelling.
- **Ignoring incumbent reaction.** Incumbents with high share can cut price or bundle service locally. Model at least one aggressive reaction case.
- **Sunk-cost momentum.** A market study already paid for, a country manager already hired. These are reasons to decide quickly, never reasons to enter.
- **Optimistic ramp.** Entry plans routinely assume faster ramp-up than comparable entries achieved. Use an outside view of comparable entries where data exists; otherwise widen the range and say so.
- **No kill or exit criteria.** Define before entry what result by when triggers exit, and what exit costs.
- **Forgetting the alternative.** An entry that clears its own hurdle can still lose to a better use of the same capital.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Alder Pumps asks how to enter Poland as part of reaching EUR 200m revenue by 2030. Sharpened question: "Can Poland deliver a meaningful share of the EUR 20m gap by 2030 within the EUR 20m capital limit, and better than the alternatives?" Kill criterion set upfront: drop Poland if the leading local players together hold more than 70% of the addressable segment.

Findings: the addressable segment for Alder's pump range is about EUR 60m. Two local players hold 78%, about EUR 47m, with long-standing service relationships at the large utilities. The remaining 22%, about EUR 13m, is split among several importers. A realistic Alder share of that open segment by 2030, through a distributor and later a small sales office, is about a third, giving under EUR 5m revenue. The kill criterion is met. In the same period, service contracts on the home installed base can add about EUR 12.5m for EUR 6m investment. Recommendation: do not enter Poland now; revisit in 2029 if a local player weakens or the service offer creates a sales argument incumbents cannot match.

## Playbook

How to run this case step by step. Steps 1 to 4 are specific to this case type; the shared engagement procedure covers the rest.

### Step 1: Sharpen the question

Clients often arrive with the market chosen. Reopen "whether" without losing their trust. Ask, in small groups:
- What minimum revenue and margin by which year would make this worth the effort? Without a minimum, every market looks worth entering.
- How much capital and management time will you commit, and for how long before you would pull out?
- Why this market, and why now? Is a competitor move, a customer request or a sunk study driving it?
- Which segment exactly: products, customer types, regions inside the country?
- What has already been spent or promised (study, hire, distributor talks)?
- If several markets: which criteria and weights decide the ranking? Agree them before scoring.

Write the decision question in this form: "Should we enter <market> by <year>, and if so how, given it must deliver at least <x> revenue and <y> margin within <capital> and <time>?" Write the kill criteria next to it now, before any analysis.

### Step 2: Instantiate the issue tree

Adapt the skeleton in the case note; set each threshold with the client.

| Branch | Hypothesis (example form) | Kill criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Attractive: size | Addressable segment is at least <x> and growing | Bottom-up SAM below the minimum bar |
| Attractive: structure | No player holds more than <y>% of the addressable segment | Local leaders together above <y>% with entrenched relationships |
| Attractive: barriers | Certification and local rules are passable within <time> | Approval takes longer than the window or needs local content we cannot provide |
| Can win: need | Target buyers have the job our offer does and would switch | Buyers solve it well today; no switching trigger found in interviews |
| Can win: position | We beat incumbents on at least one buying criterion that matters | No advantage once local price, service and channel are included |
| How: mode | One mode reaches revenue within budget and can be exited cheaply | Every mode needs more capital or time than allowed |
| Pays | Base case reaches payback within <n> years | Base case misses the minimum bar, or the low case loses more than <x> |
| Beats alternatives | Entry returns more per euro than the best alternative use | The core or another lever earns more for the same capital |

The last branch is the one teams forget. Keep it.

### Step 3: Run the deciding analyses, in order

Run the fastest kill tests first. Each analysis ends by marking its hypothesis confirmed, killed or reshaped; stop early when a kill criterion trips. If your agent can run sub-tasks in parallel, run 3a to 3c in parallel; otherwise in order.

a. **Addressable market.** [Market Sizing](market-sizing.md) top-down and bottom-up, reconciled with [Triangulation](triangulation.md). Size what the client can serve, not the headline market.
b. **Market structure.** [Five Forces](five-forces.md) and share data. High concentration among local players is often the fastest kill.
c. **Customer need and switching.** Buyer interviews ([Interview Guide](interview-guide.md)), [Jobs to Be Done](jobs-to-be-done.md), and [Customer Segmentation](customer-segmentation.md) to pick the first segment.
d. **Competitors and their reaction.** [Competitor Profiling](competitor-profiling.md) for the top players, including how each would answer an entrant.
e. **Entry modes.** Compare at least two modes on cost, speed, control and exit cost; [Value Chain](value-chain.md) shows which activities must be local. Where the input has no figures for a mode, fill each cell directionally against the costed mode, derived from the case's own cost structure and labelled as an assumption; never leave a mode "not costed".
f. **Entry business case.** [Business Case](business-case.md) with a ramp built from named customer types, channel capacity and win rates; [Unit Economics](unit-economics.md) per customer.
g. **Stress test.** [Scenario Planning](scenario-planning.md) with at least one aggressive incumbent reaction; [Sensitivity Analysis](sensitivity-analysis.md) on ramp and price.
h. **Alternative use of capital.** Same money and management time spent on the core or the next-best lever. Compare EBIT (or value) per euro of capital for entry and alternative, and grade the alternative's figures by the same evidence standard as the entry case: a management estimate stays an estimate to test.

### Step 4: Run the trap checks

Answer each with yes or no and fix every no before writing:
- [ ] Share is built bottom-up from customers, channel capacity and win rates, not "1% of a big market".
- [ ] Customers, channels and price levels were tested in the target market, not copied from home.
- [ ] At least one aggressive incumbent reaction is modelled.
- [ ] Sunk costs (study, hire, talks) play no role in the recommendation.
- [ ] The ramp is compared with comparable entries, or the range is widened and the missing comparison named.
- [ ] Money already spent (studies, trials, prior investments) is called sunk and plays no role in the decision.
- [ ] Exit criteria and exit cost are written down before entry. Exit cost = cash burned up to the exit checkpoint (from the business case) plus wind-down cost (lease, severance, customer handover) as a flagged range; an unknown wind-down cost is an assumption, not a blank.
- [ ] The entry is compared with the best alternative use of the same capital.

### Step 5: Answer page

Use the output template in [Market Entry](market-entry.md) for the body, wrapped in the shared format below. The answer is enter, do not enter, or enter later under named conditions, with mode and expected size.

- A "do not enter" is a full answer. Say what would make you revisit it and when.

### Limits
