# Five Forces

## What it is

A way to explain why an industry earns the returns it does, by assessing five pressures on profit: rivalry among incumbents, threat of entry, threat of substitutes, buyer power and supplier power. It works at industry level. It informs decisions about where to compete: enter, stay, exit, or which segment to play in. It cannot tell you whether a specific company will win there, what to charge, or how big the market is.

## When to use

- Market entry: is the target industry structurally able to pay back the entry cost?
- Acquisition screening: is the target's margin a property of its industry or of the target?
- Explaining a margin squeeze that hits all competitors, not only the client.
- Choosing between two segments of the same broad market that look similar in size.

## When NOT to use

- **The question is about one company's performance against peers.** Five forces explains the industry average, not the gap to it. Use [Competitor Profiling](competitor-profiling.md) and [Cost Benchmarking](cost-benchmarking.md).
- **The industry boundary cannot be drawn.** Platforms and ecosystems where one firm is supplier, rival and customer at once make the forces circular. Map the ecosystem's money flows first, then apply the forces per profit pool.
- **The real uncertainty is demand.** If the question is "how many will buy", use [Market Sizing](market-sizing.md) and [Scenario Planning](scenario-planning.md).
- **Structure is changing faster than the decision horizon.** Regulation or technology shifts make a snapshot misleading. Run it per scenario or skip it.
- **As a slide filler.** Five boxes rated "medium" inform nothing. If no force would change the recommendation, drop the analysis.

## Step by step

1. **Define the industry boundary.** Input: the decision. Output: one sentence naming product scope and geography, for example "aftermarket service for installed industrial pumps in DACH". Test: would a buyer switch between products inside the boundary, and do the same competitors appear? Wrong boundaries are the main source of wrong conclusions.
2. **Name the players in each force.** Input: the boundary. Output: a named list of incumbents, likely entrants, substitutes, buyer groups and supplier groups.
3. **Assess each force on its drivers, with evidence.** Rivalry: concentration, growth, fixed-cost share, exit barriers. Entry: scale needs, access to channels, switching costs, regulation. Substitutes: price-performance of alternatives, switching cost. Buyers: concentration, share of their cost, ability to integrate backward. Suppliers: concentration, uniqueness, switching cost. Output: one rating per force with the two or three drivers that set it and their evidence.
4. **Check against actual profitability.** Input: margins or returns of industry players over several years. Output: a statement whether observed returns fit the structure. A mismatch means a force is misjudged or the boundary is wrong.
5. **Identify the forces that are moving.** Output: which force is likely to strengthen or weaken over the decision horizon, and why.
6. **Write the implication for the decision.** Output: one sentence per force that matters, saying what it means for the client's choice.

## Inputs needed

- The decision the analysis serves.
- Competitor list with approximate shares; margin data for several industry players.
- Buyer and supplier structure: number, size, purchasing behaviour.
- Interviews with customers, distributors or former industry staff for the soft drivers.

## Output produced

- A table of five forces with rating, drivers, evidence and trend.
- A short judgement on structural attractiveness, checked against observed returns.
- Two or three implications for the decision.

## Common mistakes

- **No boundary.** "The pump market" mixes new equipment, spare parts and service, which have different buyers and rivals. The ratings then mean nothing.
- **Company-level ratings.** "Buyer power is high for us" mixes the client's weak position into the industry analysis. Keep the client out until step 6.
- **Ratings without drivers.** "Supplier power: high" with no evidence is an opinion.
- **Five equal boxes.** One or two forces usually explain most of the margin. Say which.
- **No link to returns.** If the analysis says "unattractive" while incumbents earn high margins for a decade, the analysis is wrong, not the industry.
- **Ignoring complements.** Products that raise the value of the industry's offer can matter as much as substitutes.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Alder Pumps considers a service-contract offer for its installed base. The team draws the boundary as "contracted maintenance for installed industrial pumps in DACH", separate from new pump sales.

- **Rivalry, medium:** independent service shops compete locally on price, but few can guarantee 48-hour response across a region.
- **Entry, low to medium:** parts access and pump-specific know-how protect the original maker; independents can serve older, simpler pumps.
- **Substitutes, medium:** in-house maintenance teams at large plants; run-to-failure for low-criticality pumps.
- **Buyer power, medium:** buyers are fragmented plant operators, but large chemical groups tender service centrally.
- **Supplier power, low:** parts are mainly Alder's own.

Check: the leading competitors report higher margins in service than in new equipment, consistent with the ratings. Implication: the service profit pool is structurally more attractive than new pumps, with in-house maintenance as the main threat. The offer should target mid-size plants without their own teams.
