# Problem Definition

## Purpose

Turn a vague briefing into one precise question that a named person will decide on. A sharp problem statement decides what is in scope, what counts as success and which analyses are worth doing. A weak one makes every later step look busy and land nowhere.

## Entry criteria

- A briefing exists, however vague.
- Someone can answer questions about the business, the decision and its constraints.

## Step by step

1. **Restate the briefing** in one sentence, in the client's words. Do not improve it yet.
2. **Find the decision.** Ask: "What will you do differently depending on our answer?" If there is no decision, there is no project, only research. Write the decision as a choice between options.
3. **Name the decision maker** and who else can block or delay it (board, works council, regulator, key customer).
4. **Set success criteria.** Make them measurable and dated: a number, a unit, a year. Add the guardrails the answer must not break (margin floor, capital limit, headcount, brand).
5. **Draw the scope.** List explicitly what is in and what is out: geographies, business units, time horizon, options the client has already ruled out and why.
6. **List constraints and givens.** Budget, timing of the decision, data that exists or does not, politically fixed facts.
7. **Ask the "why" chain** two or three times. "Why does flat revenue matter?" often reveals the real issue (a financing covenant, a succession plan, an investor exit).
8. **Write the problem statement** as one question in the form: *What should [decision maker] do about [issue] to achieve [measurable outcome] by [date], given [constraints]?*
9. **Test it** against the exit criteria below. Then get explicit agreement from the decision maker before any analysis starts.

If the user cannot answer a step, record a flagged assumption and move on. Return to it once data arrives.

## Inputs needed

- The briefing.
- Answers from the client or user to the questions in steps 2 to 7.
- Any prior strategy documents, board papers or budgets that define targets.

## Output produced

- A one-page problem statement (template below).
- A list of flagged assumptions to verify.

## Exit criteria

- The question is one sentence and ends with a question mark.
- It names a decision, a decision maker, a measurable outcome and a date.
- Scope lists at least one thing explicitly out.
- A smart outsider reading only this page would know what the final answer has to look like.
- The decision maker agreed to it, or the user confirmed it on their behalf.

## Common mistakes

- **Topic instead of question.** "Digital strategy" is a topic. "Should we move 30% of sales to direct online channels by 2028?" is a question.
- **Solution baked into the question.** "How do we launch an app?" assumes the app is the answer. Ask what the app is meant to achieve.
- **Unmeasurable success.** "Become market leader" without a metric, market definition or date cannot be tested.
- **Too broad.** Everything in scope means nothing gets analysed properly.
- **Wrong owner.** Framing the question for the person who wrote the briefing instead of the person who decides.
- **Skipping agreement.** Starting analysis on a frame the decision maker never confirmed.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Briefing from the CFO of Alder Pumps: "Revenue has been flat for three years. We want to grow." Asking what decision follows reveals that the board sets the 2027 investment budget in March and the founder family wants growth before a planned generational handover. The why chain shows the real concern: without growth, the family fears a sale at a low valuation. Final statement: "Which two growth moves should the Alder Pumps board fund in the March 2027 budget to reach EUR 200m revenue by 2030 (from EUR 180m) without lowering EBIT margin below 9%, given a capital limit of EUR 20m and no acquisitions?" Out of scope: acquisitions, new product categories outside pumps.

## Output template

```markdown
# Problem statement: <client>

**Question:** What should <decision maker> do about <issue> to achieve <measurable outcome> by <date>, given <constraints>?

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Decision | <the choice to be made, with the options on the table> |
| Decision maker | <name or role>; can block: <roles> |
| Success criteria | <metric, target, date> |
| Guardrails | <what the answer must not break> |
| In scope | |
| Out of scope | <and why> |
| Constraints | <budget, time, data, politics> |
| Decision date | |

**Why this matters now:** <one or two sentences from the why chain>

**Flagged assumptions to verify:**
1.

**Agreed by:** <name/role>, <date>
```
