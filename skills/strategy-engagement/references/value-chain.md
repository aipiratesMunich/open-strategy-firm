# Value Chain

## What it is

A breakdown of a firm into the activities that create its product and support it: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service, plus support activities such as procurement, technology, people and infrastructure. Costs and sources of customer value are assigned to each activity. It informs decisions about where to cut, invest, outsource or differentiate. It cannot tell you whether the market values the result, or what the industry's profit potential is.

## When to use

- A cost or margin problem where the team needs to know which activities drive it.
- Make-or-buy and outsourcing questions.
- Comparing cost structure with a competitor activity by activity.
- Testing whether a claimed differentiation is backed by a specific activity.

## When NOT to use

- **The problem is the market, not the firm.** Falling prices across the industry are a [Five Forces](five-forces.md) question.
- **The business has no linear flow.** Platforms and marketplaces create value by matching sides. Map the transactions and network effects instead.
- **Cost data cannot be allocated to activities.** If the ledger only shows cost types (material, personnel), start with a [Profit Tree](profit-tree.md) and a cost-type split, then allocate the largest pools.
- **The question is a single process.** A detailed process map is more useful than a firm-wide chain.
- **As a generic picture.** The standard nine boxes copied onto a slide add nothing. Redefine the activities to fit the client's business.

## Step by step

1. **Define activities for this business.** Input: org chart, process documentation. Output: 6 to 10 activities that together cover the firm, named in the client's terms.
2. **Allocate cost.** Input: P&L and cost-centre data. Output: cost per activity in currency and as share of revenue, with allocation rules written down. Allocated costs must sum to total costs.
3. **Locate customer value.** Input: customer interviews, win-loss data. Output: for each activity, whether it drives what customers pay for.
4. **Compare.** Input: benchmarks or competitor estimates. Output: activity-level gaps in cost share or performance ([Cost Benchmarking](cost-benchmarking.md)).
5. **Check linkages.** Output: cases where saving in one activity raises cost or lowers value in another, for example cheaper parts raising service cost.
6. **Draw implications.** Output: activities to fix, invest in, outsource or leave, each with the cost or value at stake.

## Inputs needed

- P&L and cost-centre data, headcount by function.
- Process descriptions; interviews with heads of each function.
- Customer view of what drives purchase.
- Benchmarks or competitor data where available.

## Output produced

- Cost by activity, reconciled to the P&L.
- Value drivers by activity.
- A ranked list of activity-level opportunities with value at stake.

## Common mistakes

- **Allocation that does not reconcile.** If activity costs do not sum to total cost, the analysis cannot be trusted.
- **Textbook activities.** "Inbound logistics" for a software firm. Use the client's real activities.
- **Cost only.** Cutting an activity that customers pay for destroys margin. Show value next to cost.
- **Ignoring linkages.** Each activity optimised alone raises total cost.
- **No benchmark.** "Sales costs 12% of revenue" means nothing without a comparison.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Alder Pumps, revenue EUR 180m, EBIT 9.5% (EUR 17.1m). Cost by activity as share of revenue: procurement and materials 45%, manufacturing 20%, sales and distribution 12%, service 5%, R&D 3%, overheads 5.5%. Total 90.5%, EBIT 9.5%.

Customer interviews show plant managers value fast response after a failure more than pump efficiency. Service is the smallest cost block and today runs as reactive repair. Linkage: Alder has no record of which installed pumps are at risk, so field engineers travel without parts and repeat visits. Implication: invest in service as an activity (planned maintenance, parts stocking, installed-base data) before cutting cost elsewhere. Manufacturing cost is in line with the benchmark and is not the priority.
