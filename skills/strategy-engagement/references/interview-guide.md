# Interview Guide

## Purpose

Get evidence from people that data cannot provide: how customers actually decide, how a market really works, why internal numbers look the way they do. Interviews are the fastest test for many hypotheses and the most common source of false confidence. A guide keeps them focused on past behaviour and facts, not on opinions and politeness.

## Entry criteria

- A hypothesis list exists; each interview targets named hypotheses.
- It is clear which type of interview is needed: customer, expert, or internal stakeholder.

## Step by step

1. **Pick the hypotheses** this interview should test. Two to four per interview, no more.
2. **Choose interviewees** to cover different perspectives: users and buyers, satisfied and lost customers, incumbents and challengers. Aim for disagreement, not confirmation.
3. **Write the guide** with three parts:
   - Opening (2 minutes): purpose, how notes will be used, confidentiality.
   - Core (most of the time): open questions about concrete past events. "Walk me through the last time you ...", "What happened next?", "What did that cost you?"
   - Close: "What should I have asked?", "Who else should I talk to?", and a concrete ask if relevant (data, an introduction).
4. **Translate each hypothesis into behaviour questions.** Not "Would you buy a service contract?" but "When did a pump last fail? What did you do? What did it cost? Who decided?"
5. **Avoid leading questions.** Do not mention your hypothesis, your client's product or the answer you hope for until the end, if at all.
6. **Run the interview.** Listen more than talk. Follow surprises. Ask for numbers and examples whenever a claim is general ("usually", "always", "a lot").
7. **Write notes within the hour.** Separate three things: what was said (quotes, facts), what was observed, and what you infer. Tag each note with the hypothesis it affects.
8. **Update the hypothesis list** after every few interviews. Stop interviewing on a hypothesis when new interviews stop changing the picture.

For expert interviews (former employees, industry specialists, distributors): ask about market structure, economics and how competitors behave. Never ask an expert for confidential information from a current or former employer.

## Inputs needed

- Hypothesis list and kill criteria.
- Interviewee list with role and why each was chosen.
- For an agent: if live interviews are impossible, the user supplies transcripts or notes; otherwise mark the hypothesis as untested.

## Output produced

- Interview guide per interviewee type.
- Structured notes per interview.
- Summary across interviews: per hypothesis, how many interviews support, contradict, or add nuance, with representative evidence.

## Exit criteria

- Every targeted hypothesis was discussed in enough interviews that the last few added nothing new.
- Notes separate fact from inference and are tagged to hypotheses.
- At least one interviewee was chosen because they were likely to disagree.

## Common mistakes

- **Pitching instead of listening.** Explaining the idea and then asking if they like it produces compliments, not evidence.
- **Future hypotheticals.** "Would you pay for X?" answers are weak predictors of behaviour. Past actions and money spent are strong.
- **Talking only to fans.** Interviewing the client's best customers and concluding the market loves the product.
- **Counting opinions as data.** Eight of ten interviewees "liking" something is not a market share.
- **Notes from memory.** Writing up days later mixes what was said with what the interviewer wanted to hear.
- **Unethical expert asks.** Requesting a competitor's confidential numbers puts the client at legal risk.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

To test H2 for Alder Pumps (customers will pay at least EUR 900 per pump per year), the team interviews 12 plant managers: 6 Alder customers, 3 who left for a competitor, 3 who never bought Alder. The weak question "Would you buy a service contract for EUR 900?" is replaced by "Tell me about the last unplanned pump failure", followed by "How long was the line down? What did that cost? Who repaired it, and what did you pay?" Nine of twelve describe a failure in the last 18 months; reported downtime costs range from EUR 4,000 to EUR 40,000 per incident. Two of the three lost customers say they left because Alder's repair response took more than a week. The summary marks H2 as supported, with a nuance: response time matters more than price, which reshapes the service offer.

## Output template

```markdown
# Interview guide: <interviewee type>

**Hypotheses tested:** H<n>, H<n>
**Target interviewees:** <roles, number, why these (include likely dissenters)>

## Opening (2 min)
- Purpose, confidentiality, how notes are used

## Core questions
1. Walk me through the last time you <behaviour linked to H<n>>.
2. What happened next? Who was involved? What did it cost?
3. <follow-up probes for numbers and examples>

## Close
- What should I have asked?
- Who else should I talk to?

---

# Interview notes: <role>, <date>

| Said (facts, quotes) | Observed | Inferred | Hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|

---

# Interview summary

| Hypothesis | Interviews | Support | Contradict | Nuance | Representative evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
```
