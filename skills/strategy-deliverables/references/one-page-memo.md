# One-Page Memo

## What it is

A single page of prose that states an answer, the reasons and the next step, written to be read without a meeting. It forces complete sentences and explicit links between points, which slides let the author skip. It is the right format for decisions that a small group reads and acts on.

## When NOT to use

- **The proof is visual.** When the argument depends on several charts (trends, benchmarks, a waterfall), a short deck shows it faster. Use [Deck Structure](deck-structure.md).
- **Large or mixed audiences** in a presented meeting, such as a steering committee with ten members. They need a shared screen and a pre-read deck ([Steering Committee and Final Presentation](steerco-and-final-presentation.md)).
- **The question is still open.** A memo reads as a conclusion. For an open question, use a short options paper or a workshop.
- **More than one decision.** Two unrelated decisions in one memo means one gets ignored. Write two memos.

When a memo beats a deck:
- One decision, one owner or a small group.
- The reader has time to read and will decide alone or by email.
- The reasoning is causal ("because ... therefore ...") rather than a set of exhibits.
- Speed matters: a memo takes hours, a deck takes days.

## Standard

- **One page**, including the decision request.
- **Subject line states the answer**, not the topic.
- **First paragraph:** the recommendation and the decision requested, in two or three sentences.
- **Second part:** three to five reasons, each a sentence with its key number, in order of importance ([Pyramid Principle](pyramid-principle.md)).
- **Risks:** what would change the answer.
- **Next steps:** who does what by when.
- **Plain language**, no slide-style fragments, no undefined abbreviations.
- **At most one small table or figure**, and only if it carries a reason.
- **Sources** in a footnote line; detail as an attachment.

## Step by step

1. Write the answer as the subject line.
2. Write the opening paragraph: recommendation, decision requested, deadline.
3. List the reasons from the synthesis ([Synthesis](synthesis.md)); keep the three to five strongest.
4. Give each reason its number and source.
5. Add risks and next steps.
6. Cut to one page. Remove background the reader already knows.
7. Read it as the recipient: could they reply "approved" without asking a question?

## Common mistakes

- **Topic as subject line.** "Pricing review." The reader cannot triage it.
- **Story told in order of work.** "We first looked at..." Struck: the reader wants the answer, not the diary.
- **Slide bullets pasted into a memo.** Fragments without verbs lose the links between reasons, which is the point of a memo.
- **Overflow to page two.** The decision request ends up on a page nobody reads.
- **No ask.** A memo that informs but requests nothing gets filed.

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Weak subject line: "Update on pricing analysis". Body opens with three paragraphs on the benchmarking method.

Strong:

**Subject: Raise Alder list prices by 4% from July 2027, phased for the top 20 accounts**

To the managing director, head of sales. We recommend a 4% list price increase from 1 July 2027, worth about EUR 7m a year. Please approve at the March 2027 board meeting so price lists can go to distributors in April.

Reasons: Alder's list prices are 6% below the two main competitors for comparable pumps, so a 4% increase still leaves Alder below both. In interviews, customers ranked response time and reliability above price. The increase protects the EBIT margin floor of 9% while the service offer ramps up.

Risk: key accounts may push back. We propose phasing the increase over two steps for the top 20 accounts.

Next steps: head of sales prepares account-level price lists by 28 February 2027; finance updates the 2027 budget by 31 March 2027.

Source: distributor price lists, Q2 2026; customer interviews, 2026.

## Output template

```markdown
**Subject: <the recommendation in one line>**

To: <decision maker(s)>. We recommend <action>, worth <effect>. Please <decision> by <date> so that <reason for deadline>.

Reasons:
1. <Reason with number and comparison.>
2. <Reason.>
3. <Reason.>

Risks: <what could go wrong and how it is handled; what would change the answer.>

Next steps: <owner> <action> by <date>; <owner> <action> by <date>.

Sources: <source, date>. Attachments: <detail>.
```
