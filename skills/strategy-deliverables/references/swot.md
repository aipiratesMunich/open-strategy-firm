# SWOT

## What it is

A two-by-two summary of internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats. Used well, it condenses finished analysis into the few facts that drive a decision and states what they imply. It informs nothing on its own: it collects conclusions, it does not produce them. It cannot tell you which factor matters most, how large it is, or what to do about it.

## When to use

- As a one-page summary at the end of analysis, where every item points to evidence already produced.
- To brief a board on the position behind a recommendation, turned into prioritised implications.
- As a check during [Synthesis](synthesis.md): has any major internal or external factor been left out of the storyline?

## When NOT to use

- **At the start of a project.** A SWOT before analysis lists opinions. Start with problem definition and an issue tree.
- **To find growth options.** Use ansoff matrix and [Market Sizing](market-sizing.md).
- **To judge industry attractiveness.** Use five forces.
- **To diagnose profit.** Use [Profit Tree](profit-tree.md) and [Value Chain](value-chain.md).
- **When no decision is on the table.** Without a decision there is no test for relevance, and the list grows without limit.

### Why SWOT is usually misused

An empirical review of SWOT in practice found long lists, typically around forty factors, written in general terms, not prioritised, not checked against evidence, and not used in the later strategy work at all (hill westbrook 1997). Three patterns explain it:

1. **Unprioritised lists.** Every item gets one bullet, so a minor weakness weighs as much as a threat to the core business. The reader cannot see what matters.
2. **Internal and external mixed.** "Strong brand in a growing market" puts a strength and an opportunity in one item. A strength is something the company controls; an opportunity exists whether or not the company acts. Mixing them hides whether the company can actually capture the opportunity.
3. **No link to a decision.** Items are not tied to a choice the client must make, so nothing follows from the chart. It becomes decoration.

If SWOT is used at all, use it as a summary after analysis, keep it short, attach evidence to every item, and convert it into prioritised implications: which strength meets which opportunity, which weakness exposes the company to which threat, and what the decision should be.

## Step by step

1. **State the decision.** Input: problem statement. Output: the choice the SWOT must inform, in one sentence.
2. **Pull findings from finished analyses.** Input: outputs of five forces, [Profit Tree](profit-tree.md), [Value Chain](value-chain.md), [Market Sizing](market-sizing.md), interviews. Output: candidate items, each with a source analysis.
3. **Sort strictly.** Strengths and weaknesses: under the company's control, relative to competitors. Opportunities and threats: external, independent of the company. Output: four lists with no mixed items.
4. **Filter by relevance and size.** Keep only items that would change the decision, each quantified where possible. Output: no more than three to five items per box.
5. **Cross the boxes.** Output: implications pairing internal and external items (strength meets opportunity, weakness meets threat).
6. **Rank the implications.** Input: value at stake, urgency. Output: a ranked list that feeds the recommendation and [Storyline and Ghost Deck](storyline.md).

## Inputs needed

- A defined decision.
- Completed analyses with quantified findings.
- Competitor data to judge whether a strength is relative or only internal pride.

## Output produced

- A short SWOT, every item evidenced and quantified where possible.
- A ranked list of implications linked to the decision. The implications carry the message; the grid is supporting material.

## Common mistakes

- **Forty bullets.** Signals that no one decided what matters. Strike it and ask for the top three per box.
- **Strengths without comparison.** "Good quality" is a strength only if customers see it as better than competitors. Otherwise it is table stakes.
- **Opportunities that are actions.** "Enter Poland" is an option, not an opportunity. The opportunity is the market condition; the option belongs in the recommendation.
- **Unsourced items.** Each item should point to an analysis or data source (weak evidence).
- **SWOT as the final slide.** A grid with no implications leaves the reader to do the thinking ([Unanswered So-What](unanswered-so-what.md)).

## Worked example

*Fictional example. Company, figures and people are invented.*

Decision: should Alder Pumps invest EUR 6m in a service-contract offer? The first draft from a workshop had 38 items. After analysis, the summary keeps eight:

- **Strengths:** installed base of 60,000 pumps; parts and pump know-how competitors lack.
- **Weaknesses:** no installed-base register; field service sized for reactive repair only.
- **Opportunities:** 52% of the installed base is older than eight years without a service contract; plant managers state willingness to pay about EUR 1,000 per pump per year for 48-hour response.
- **Threats:** in-house maintenance teams at large plants; independent repair shops on price.

Ranked implications:
1. Installed base plus uncontracted older pumps → service contracts worth about EUR 12.5m revenue at 40% take-up (range EUR 8m to 15.6m). Invest.
2. Missing register plus independent shops → without the register, Alder cannot target pumps before independents do. Build the register first, inside the EUR 6m.
3. In-house teams at large plants → focus the offer on mid-size plants.
